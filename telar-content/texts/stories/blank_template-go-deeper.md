The production of the Moche stirrup spout begins with the materials needed. Including the clay with fine grained sand temper, while other less elaborate vessels recieved coarse sand. Then that clay was pressed around an existing vessel or a mold to make the chamber, leaving an opening above. The artisan would smooth the interoior in the mold to prevent warping, while the upper portion was then closed using a coiling technique with clay loops added and smothed. This process is seen through visible clay lumps at the top of the vessels. The spout was made seperately by wrapping clay around wooden rods, after sealing the seams and joining the sections together the rods were taken out and the clay was bent into the stirrup shape. This was then added to the body of the vessel by punching holes into the clay and then slitting the two together with a swab stick. This too left extra clay internally around the shoulders of the vessel. Decorated vessels were stamped against the clay, while supporting the inside with ones hand. In Moche ceramics usually only one design was used per vessel and the impressions were always even throughout the piece. After wards the vessel was smoothed with abrassive stones or shells the rubbed with cloth or leather, so that the red and white paints could be applied. Right before firing the vessel was again polished with a hard stone and taken to the open pit to fire stacked ontop of other pots. Due to the atmosphere at firing the color of the pots resulted in the red pigment we see today. It is important to note the sophisticated and standardized process the Moche developed to produce artistry that was widespread and held a distinct cultural mark on Ancient Andean pottery
